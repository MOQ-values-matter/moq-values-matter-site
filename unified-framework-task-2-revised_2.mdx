## Purpose of This Document

This document supersedes the earlier Task 2 framework construction. It is a revised version, not an addition, because the revisions touch the framework's foundational primitives and the document's clarity requires the framework to be presented whole rather than patched.

The framework is built on a single foundational commitment that has been refined through stress-testing: **patterns are the primitive, and every pattern has two aspects — a static face and a becoming face — neither more fundamental than the other.** Patterns can have internal compositional structure of arbitrary depth. A pattern can be composed of other patterns standing in mutual relation; those constituent patterns can themselves be composed of finer-grain patterns; and so on. There is one entity-category — *pattern* — and patterns exist at every compositional scale.

The vocabulary *consonance* and *dissonance* survives as descriptive terms for the character of mutual constitution among a pattern's constituents. A pattern whose constituents support each other's realisation is consonant in its internal composition. A pattern whose constituents interfere with each other's realisation is dissonant in its internal composition. Patterns can persist in either condition, though dissonant patterns persist on borrowed substrate and accumulate structural costs that eventually exceed what their substrate can absorb.

This framework is exploratory. It does not modify Canon. Promotion of any part of it to Canon requires the full procedure: stress-testing, scrutiny, explicit promotion, declaration. The framework lives in essays until it earns its way out.

<a id="section-1-foundational-commitments"></a>
## 1. Foundational Commitments

The framework's commitments are not negotiable within the framework itself. Anyone working with the framework is working within these commitments; anyone who finds them unworkable is working with a different framework.

---

<a id="commitment-1-value-as-primary"></a>
### 1.1 Value is Primary

Value is not a property attached to objects, nor a subjective opinion about them. Value is the primary reality from which what we call objects and what we call subjects both arise. This is Pirsig's foundational claim, and the framework inherits it without modification.

What this means for the framework: every primitive the framework defines is a primitive *of value*, not a primitive of matter, mind, behaviour, or representation. The framework does not describe "value patterns in addition to physical patterns and mental patterns"; it describes patterns of value, and what is conventionally called physical or mental is itself an account of how value patterns are organised.

---

<a id="commitment-2-pattern-as-primitive"></a>
### 1.2 The Pattern is the Primitive — and the Only Primitive Entity-Category

Among the candidates surveyed in Task 1, the framework adopts **the pattern** as its foundational unit. Not the event, not the field, not the substance, not the function — the pattern.

A pattern is what persists. A pattern is also what is happening, right now, as it continues to persist. The pattern is not a thing that has activity; the pattern *is* a continuity-of-realisation. There is no patternless realisation, and there is no realisation-less pattern. Pattern-being and pattern-becoming are not two stages of pattern existence; they are two aspects of the same thing, named separately only because language is built that way.

This is the framework's central philosophical move and the one that does the most work. It refuses the standard state/operator separation at the foundational level — the move that the formal-mathematical traditions make and that creates the conceptual difficulty of treating becoming as something other than primitive. The framework permits state/operator analysis as a tool for describing already-stabilised patterns, but it does not allow the analysis to claim that pattern-as-state and pattern-as-becoming are categorially distinct.

**Pattern is the only entity-category.** The framework does not have separate entity-categories for individual patterns and for clusters of patterns. A cluster of mutually-constitutive patterns is itself a pattern — a pattern with internal compositional structure. This applies uniformly across strata and across compositional scales. The principle: *whatever the framework recognises as a stable, recurrent value-form is a pattern, regardless of its scale or its internal compositional depth.*

---

<a id="commitment-3-two-aspects"></a>
### 1.3 Every Pattern Has Two Aspects

Every pattern has a **static face** and a **becoming face**.

The static face is the pattern's invariance: what would make us say "the same pattern is still here" across time, across instances, across substrates. The static face is what allows the pattern to be recognised, referred to, and analysed.

The becoming face is the pattern's continuous realisation: what is happening, right now, that constitutes the pattern's persistence. The becoming face is what makes the pattern an actual thing rather than a possibility.

These two faces are not separable. A pattern with only a static face is an abstraction — a pattern that *would* be a pattern if it were realised, but is not. A pattern with only a becoming face is a noise — a happening that does not constitute a recognisable invariance. Real patterns have both.

This is the framework's working-through of the Parmenides–Heraclitus problem. Parmenides was right that pure flux is unintelligible. Heraclitus was right that pure stasis is unreal. The framework's answer is that patterns *are* the synthesis: every pattern is at once stable and in motion, and the appearance of contradiction comes from trying to choose one over the other.

---

<a id="commitment-4-compositional-scale"></a>
### 1.4 Patterns Have Internal Compositional Structure of Arbitrary Depth

A pattern can be composed of other patterns in mutual constitutive relation. Those constituent patterns can themselves be composed of finer-grain patterns. There is no privileged scale at which the framework operates; patterns exist at every scale of composition, and the framework describes them uniformly.

In the biological stratum, a cell is a pattern. A tissue is a pattern composed of cells in mutual relation. An organ is a pattern composed of tissues. An organism is a pattern composed of organs. Each is a biological-stratum pattern. What varies is compositional scale, not entity-category.

In the social stratum, the same holds. Justice is a pattern. Governance is a pattern composed of justice, authority, legitimacy, responsibility, sanction, adjudication in mutual relation. A political order is a pattern composed of governance, economy, religion, language, education in mutual relation. A civilisation is a pattern composed of political orders, languages, traditions, technologies in mutual relation. Each is a social-stratum pattern, regardless of compositional scale.

This is a substantial structural commitment. It means the Atlas catalogues patterns at all compositional scales without privileging any scale. A pattern entry for justice and a pattern entry for governance and a pattern entry for civilisation all use the same structural apparatus (profile, coherence analysis, substrate, modulation, field, bearer modes, disposition). The methodology may need to adapt to scale — describing civilisation empirically is harder than describing justice empirically — but the ontology does not.

The principle has a stabilising effect on the framework. It prevents the proliferation of entity-categories that would otherwise creep in as analysis encounters new compositional scales. It also matches the biological analogy: biology does not need separate entity-categories for cells, tissues, organs, organisms; it treats them all as biological structures at different scales, studied with appropriate methodology at each scale. The framework treats value patterns the same way.

---

<a id="commitment-5-dynamic-quality"></a>
### 1.5 Dynamic Quality is Not a Mechanism

Dynamic Quality is the pre-conceptual source of novelty in patterns, recognisable only after it has passed into pattern form. Within the framework, Dynamic Quality has a referenceable place: it is what new patterns and new realisations come *from*, in the sense that they were not derivable from prior patterns alone.

The framework does not name Dynamic Quality as a mechanism, operator, driver, lens, or method. It does not say that Dynamic Quality *acts on* patterns, *flows through* patterns, or *is applied to* patterns. The relationship between Dynamic Quality and patterns is named **passage**: what was not yet a pattern passes into pattern form. Passage is directional and asymmetric. We can recognise that a passage has occurred (a new pattern is here that was not derivable from prior patterns); we cannot operationalise the passage itself.

This is the framework's most carefully guarded boundary. Every other primitive in the framework is operationalisable in the sense that statements using it can be checked, compared, and revised. Dynamic Quality, and the passage that produces patterns, is not. The framework refers to it only at the boundary where its non-operationalisability is the point.

---

<a id="commitment-6-fourfold-stratification"></a>
### 1.6 Patterns are Stratified into Four Domains

The framework adopts Pirsig's four levels — physical, biological, social, intellectual — under the name **strata**. The renaming is deliberate: the framework's strata are not understood as "levels of organisation" in the reductive sense, nor as "levels of abstraction" in the intellectualist sense. They are *domains of pattern-realisation*, each with its own characteristic mode of becoming, its own conditions of stability, and its own form of quality.

The four strata are ordered. Lower strata are *substrate* for higher strata: the realisation of a higher-stratum pattern depends on conditions held by patterns in the stratum it depends on. Higher strata have *conditional precedence* over lower strata: when a higher-stratum pattern is meeting its own conditions of realisation, its requirements take priority over the requirements of lower-stratum patterns it depends on.

Cross-stratum interaction is continuous and legitimate. Higher strata *modulate* lower strata — they shape, formalise, constrain, and stabilise lower-stratum realisations. Higher strata do not *originate* lower-stratum patterns — they cannot bring into existence what the lower stratum has not produced as substrate. The asymmetry of modulation-but-not-origination is the framework's account of what is sometimes called downward causation: it permits the genuine influence of higher strata on lower strata while preserving the substrate dependence in the other direction.

Compositional scale operates within each stratum. A stratum contains patterns at many scales (cells through organisms in biology; constituent virtues through civilisations in social). The compositional-scale axis is orthogonal to the stratification axis: a pattern is located by both *which stratum* it operates within and *what scale of internal composition* it has.

<a id="section-2-primitive-vocabulary"></a>
## 2. Primitive Vocabulary

The framework's primitives are the smallest set of named entities and relations from which everything else in the framework is derived. They are introduced here as definitions; their relations and behaviour are developed in subsequent sections.

---

<a id="primitive-pattern"></a>
### 2.1 Pattern

A **pattern** is a continuity-of-realisation of value. A pattern persists by continuing to be realised; what it is, is the form its realisation takes.

Every pattern has a static face (its invariance) and a becoming face (its continuous realisation). The two faces are not separable in the pattern itself; they are distinguished only in analysis.

Patterns can have internal compositional structure. A pattern composed of other patterns in mutual relation is itself a pattern, with its own profile, its own coherence conditions, its own failure modes. The composing patterns retain their own identities and have their own analysable profiles; the composed pattern is not identical with the sum of its constituents, because what makes it a pattern is the structural form of their mutual relation, not their individual presence.

A pattern is located in one stratum primarily (where its core mode of realisation occurs) and may have presence in other strata (where it modulates, is modulated, or propagates effects).

---

<a id="primitive-realisation"></a>
### 2.2 Realisation

**Realisation** is the becoming face of a pattern made concrete — what is happening, in a specific situation, as the pattern continues to be. Realisation is always *of* some pattern; there is no realisation that is not the realisation of a pattern.

A pattern's realisation is distinct from any single instance of it. A pattern is realised in many places, many times, by many bearers. The realisations are concrete; the pattern they realise is the invariance they share.

Realisation can be more or less complete, more or less coherent, more or less stable. A pattern may be realised partially, distortedly, under stress, or in degraded form. The realisation is not the pattern; it is the pattern's mode of being-here-now.

For composed patterns, realisation is the continuous mutual realisation of the constituent patterns *in their compositional relation*. The composed pattern is realised when its constituents are realised in the mutual relation that constitutes its form. When that mutual relation degrades, the composed pattern's realisation degrades, even when its constituents continue to be individually realised.

---

<a id="primitive-substrate"></a>
### 2.3 Substrate

**Substrate** is the set of conditions, drawn from lower-stratum patterns, on which a pattern's realisation depends. A pattern's realisation is possible only when its substrate conditions hold.

Substrate is structural, not causal in the ordinary sense. Substrate does not produce the higher pattern; substrate makes the higher pattern's realisation possible. Where substrate fails, the higher pattern fails to realise — not because the substrate caused the higher pattern to fail, but because the higher pattern's mode of being depended on what the substrate provided.

Substrate is asymmetric: lower-stratum patterns can be substrate for higher-stratum patterns; higher-stratum patterns cannot be substrate for lower-stratum patterns. (Higher-stratum patterns can *modulate* lower-stratum patterns, which is a different relation.)

For composed patterns, substrate operates at the level of the composed pattern as a whole, not separately for each constituent. Governance, as a composed pattern, has substrate dependencies (biological agents capable of recognising authority, physical-stratum infrastructure for communication, etc.). The constituents of governance (justice, authority, etc.) have their own substrate dependencies as patterns in their own right. The composed pattern's substrate is not the union of its constituents' substrates; it includes additional conditions that arise specifically from the compositional structure.

---

<a id="primitive-modulation"></a>
### 2.4 Modulation

**Modulation** is the relation by which a higher-stratum pattern shapes the realisation of a lower-stratum pattern. Modulation is the framework's name for legitimate cross-stratum downward influence.

Modulation can stabilise, constrain, formalise, distort, or amplify a lower-stratum pattern's realisation. What modulation cannot do is originate the lower-stratum pattern. The lower-stratum pattern must exist as substrate already; modulation operates on what is already there.

Modulation is the framework's positive account of what MOQ commitments rule out under the name "origination across levels." Cross-stratum influence is not forbidden; it is constrained to modulation. The asymmetry is what preserves the substrate-dependence relation: higher strata depend on lower strata for their realisation, but they shape the lower strata in return.

**Modulation has three kinds.** The character of a modulation is not fixed by the fact of modulation; a higher pattern can shape a lower pattern's realisation in three structurally different ways:

- *Supportive modulation* strengthens the lower pattern's own realisation — stabilising it, formalising it, making it more robust on its own terms.
- *Distorting modulation* forces the lower pattern into configurations that compromise its mode of being, for the higher pattern's benefit. The lower pattern continues to be realised, but its realisation is conscripted against its own coherence.
- *Corrective modulation* operates on the lower pattern by surfacing where the lower pattern's own profile-coherence is weak, without directly reshaping its realisation. The lower pattern's weaknesses are made visible; what follows — strengthening, transformation, or collapse — is downstream of the surfacing, not produced by it. Corrective modulation is most characteristic of intellectual patterns operating on lower-stratum substrate (an intellectual pattern testing a social pattern's representations against reality surfaces where those representations diverge, without itself reshaping the social pattern), but it is not exclusive to them.

The three kinds are part of a pattern's profile: a high-quality higher pattern tends to modulate its substrate supportively or correctively; a pattern that distorts the substrate it depends on is, in that dimension, lower quality.

**Modulation is direct or cascaded.** A higher pattern modulates the stratum immediately below it *directly*. It reaches strata further below only *through cascade* — by modulating the intermediate stratum, whose altered realisation then modulates the stratum below it in turn. This distinction matters analytically and prevents a specific error: attributing to a higher pattern a direct effect on a distant lower stratum when the effect is in fact mediated. An intellectual pattern does not, in the general case, modulate biology directly; it modulates social patterns, and biological effects follow from the social change cascading downward. The substrate asymmetry is preserved at every step of the cascade — no step originates the stratum below it; each only shapes what is already there. Direct intellectual-to-biological modulation, if it occurs at all, is narrow and exceptional; the general case of higher patterns affecting distant lower strata runs through cascade.

For composed patterns, modulation operates from the composed pattern as a whole and from the composed pattern's constituents. Both are real. The governance pattern, as a whole, modulates biological agents in distinctive ways (e.g., by inducing the agent into role-bearing). The constituent justice pattern also modulates biological agents (by specifying when restraint is required). These are not duplicate modulations; they operate at different scales of the same composed pattern's modulation activity.

---

<a id="primitive-field"></a>
### 2.5 Field

A **field** is a relational medium within which a pattern's realisations affect other patterns' realisations. Fields are not separate from patterns; they are constituted by the relations among patterns whose realisations are in mutual influence.

A field can be characterised by its bearers (what patterns are realised within it), its propagation rules (how realisations in one part of the field affect other parts), and its boundaries (where the field's influence drops off or transforms). A field is always relative — there is no universal field, only fields constituted by specific patterns in specific configurations.

The framework adopts the Circle/Ripple theory's *value field* concept in adapted form. A value field in the Circle/Ripple sense is a special case: a field constituted predominantly by social-stratum patterns with biological and physical substrates, characterised by reciprocity, trust, and enforcement dynamics. The framework's *field* is more general; value fields are one class of fields among others.

Fields are not the same as composed patterns. A field is a relational medium *between* patterns; a composed pattern is a pattern *constituted* by its constituents in mutual relation. The distinction matters: patterns can propagate effects through a field without being constituents of any composed pattern that the field belongs to.

---

<a id="primitive-stratum"></a>
### 2.6 Stratum

A **stratum** is a domain of pattern-realisation with a characteristic mode of becoming. The framework recognises four strata, in order: physical, biological, social, intellectual. The strata are not arbitrary; they correspond to genuinely different modes by which patterns realise themselves.

- The **physical stratum** is the domain of patterns whose realisation is passive persistence within conservation constraints. A physical pattern persists by being what it already is; its mode of becoming is endurance.
- The **biological stratum** is the domain of patterns whose realisation is active resistance to entropy through self-maintenance. A biological pattern persists by continuously producing the conditions of its own persistence; its mode of becoming is self-production.
- The **social stratum** is the domain of patterns whose realisation is multi-agent coordination. A social pattern persists by being repeatedly enacted across multiple agents in mutual orientation; its mode of becoming is coordination.
- The **intellectual stratum** is the domain of patterns whose realisation is symbolic operation under structural conditions of coherence, correspondence, and revisability. An intellectual pattern persists by being articulated in transmissible form, tested against evidence and argument, and revised when warranted; its mode of becoming is testable representation.

The strata are ordered. Each stratum's mode of becoming presupposes the modes of becoming of the strata below it. There is no biology without physical persistence; no sociality without biological agents; no intellect without social conditions for symbolic communication. This ordering is what substrate-dependence formalises.

Each stratum contains patterns at many compositional scales. The framework does not impose a fixed list of scales within a stratum; the scales that matter are the ones that produce stable, recurrent patterns worthy of analysis.

---

<a id="primitive-disposition"></a>
### 2.7 Disposition

A **disposition** is the becoming-shape of a pattern: what the pattern *tends toward* in its realisation. A disposition is not a goal, not a purpose, not a final cause. It is a directional tendency observable in how the pattern realises itself under varying conditions.

A pattern's disposition includes its conditions of stability (what tends to maintain its realisation), its conditions of degradation (what tends to weaken its realisation), and its sensitivities (what kinds of substrate or modulation it responds to). Disposition is the becoming-face of the pattern made analytically visible.

The framework adopts Aristotle's *dunamis* concept in adapted form here, but strips the teleology. A disposition is not oriented toward a *telos*; it is simply the shape of how the pattern realises itself, which has direction but no destination external to itself.

---

<a id="primitive-profile"></a>
### 2.8 Profile

A **profile** is a structured characterisation of a pattern: the set of statements that, taken together, specify what kind of pattern it is. A profile includes the pattern's primary stratum, its substrate dependencies, the patterns it modulates and is modulated by, its field characteristics, the patterns that participate in its internal composition (if any), the character of that internal composition (consonant, dissonant, or mixed), and its dispositions.

A profile is the framework's analytical handle on a pattern. Two patterns with identical profiles are the same pattern in different instances. Two patterns with different profiles are different patterns, even if they share a name in ordinary speech.

The profile is what would be specified in a Canon-grade Atlas entry, under the framework. The specific form of the profile — what fields it has, how detailed each is, how scored or unscored — is a downstream question for whether and how the existing Atlas structure is refactored against this framework. The framework requires that profiles exist and be coherent; it does not yet specify their precise format.

---

<a id="primitive-passage"></a>
### 2.9 Passage

**Passage** is the name for what occurs when something that was not yet a pattern enters pattern form. Passage is the framework's only term for the relationship between Dynamic Quality and the patterns Dynamic Quality is the source of.

Passage is observed only retrospectively. We recognise that passage has occurred when a pattern is present that was not derivable from prior patterns alone — when something genuinely new has stabilised. The mechanism of passage is not named because there is no mechanism; passage is the framework's marker that Dynamic Quality has done what Dynamic Quality does without the framework operationalising what that is.

The framework refers to passage at exactly two points: when a wholly new pattern stabilises (passage as origination of patterns) and when an existing pattern shifts in its disposition in a way not explicable by prior conditions (passage within an existing pattern). Beyond these references, passage does not appear in framework operations.

<a id="section-3-relations"></a>
## 3. Relations Between Primitives

The primitives are not free-standing definitions; they compose into a working framework through the relations they bear to one another.

---

<a id="relation-substrate-dependence"></a>
### 3.1 Substrate Dependence

A pattern depends on substrate. The pattern's realisation is conditioned by the realisation of the lower-stratum patterns that constitute its substrate. If substrate fails, the pattern fails to realise — not because substrate caused the failure but because the pattern's mode of being required substrate that is no longer there.

Substrate dependence runs in one direction: upward only. A pattern in stratum N depends on patterns in strata 1 through N-1, but not on patterns in strata N+1 through 4. Higher-stratum patterns are not substrate; they are modulators.

Substrate dependence is not reducibility. A pattern is not its substrate; it is what is realised given the substrate. Reducing a higher-stratum pattern to its substrate is a category error — it confuses the conditions of realisation with the pattern realised under those conditions.

---

<a id="relation-modulation"></a>
### 3.2 Modulation

A pattern in stratum N can modulate patterns in any lower stratum. The modulation operates on the lower pattern's *realisation*, not on its existence — modulation shapes how the lower pattern realises itself, not whether it exists. Origination remains forbidden.

Modulation is the framework's positive account of cross-stratum interaction. The Pirsig-derived prohibition on "explaining higher levels as configurations of lower levels" is reducibility-prohibition; the prohibition on "higher levels originating lower levels" is origination-prohibition. Both are preserved. What is no longer prohibited is modulation: higher-stratum patterns shaping lower-stratum realisations is not just permitted but constitutive of how the strata actually interact.

Examples of modulation, given for illustration only and not as Canon claims: an intellectual pattern can modulate a social pattern by articulating it into a form that can be tested. A social pattern can modulate a biological pattern by binding individual agents into coordination structures that override individual biological responses. A biological pattern can modulate a physical pattern by organising matter into self-maintaining configurations. In none of these cases does the higher pattern *originate* the lower pattern; in each case the higher pattern *shapes* what was already there.

---

<a id="relation-internal-composition"></a>
### 3.3 Internal Composition

A pattern can be composed of other patterns. The composing patterns are constituents of the composed pattern; the composed pattern is constituted by their mutual relation.

Internal composition is intra-stratum: a composed pattern's constituents are at the same stratum as the composed pattern. A social pattern composed of social patterns is governance, or a social order, or a tradition; a biological pattern composed of biological patterns is a tissue, or an organ, or an organism. The constituents and the composed pattern share a stratum.

The mutual relation among constituents has structural character that the framework describes through *consonance* and *dissonance* (developed in Section 5). Consonant internal composition is mutual support among constituents; dissonant internal composition is mutual interference. A pattern's profile includes the character of its internal composition.

Internal composition is real, not derivative. The composed pattern has properties that are not properties of any constituent: its own coherence conditions, its own failure modes, its own persistence dynamics. The framework treats the composed pattern as a pattern in its own right, with its own analytical profile, while preserving the constituents' independent analysability as patterns themselves.

---

<a id="relation-field-propagation"></a>
### 3.4 Field Propagation

A pattern's realisation in a field affects the realisations of other patterns in that field. Field propagation is how value effects move between patterns without requiring direct substrate, modulation, or compositional relations.

Propagation in a field can be amplifying (one realisation strengthens others), damping (one realisation weakens others), competitive (realisations exclude one another for limited substrate), or transformative (one realisation changes the disposition of others). The propagation rules of a field are themselves part of the field's characterisation.

The framework's account of *field-relative valuation* sits here. The same pattern, realised in two different fields, produces different value effects in each. The effects are not arbitrary; they are objective within each field, given the field's constitution. Two patterns can be field-incompatible: a pattern that propagates well in one field can propagate destructively in another. The asymmetry of field effects is structurally real, not interpretively imposed.

---

<a id="relation-bearer-modes"></a>
### 3.5 Bearer Modes

A pattern can be realised by an individual agent or by a group; these are two **modes of realisation** of the same pattern, not two different patterns. The bearer mode affects how the pattern is realised, what fields its realisations propagate through, and what its dispositions look like — but the pattern itself is the same.

Not every pattern admits every bearer mode. Some patterns are inherently agent-mode patterns: their realisation requires individual bearing and is degenerate or empty when attempted as group-mode. Some patterns are inherently group-mode patterns: their realisation requires multi-agent coordination and cannot be borne by a single agent. Many patterns admit both bearer modes, with different consequences for each.

For composed patterns, bearer mode can be complex. A composed social pattern like governance is borne by an entire field of agents in coordinated roles; its bearer mode is intrinsically group-mode at the composed-pattern level, even though many of its constituents (such as individual virtues) can be agent-mode at their own level. The framework permits this scale-dependence of bearer modes.

---

<a id="section-4-strata"></a>
## 4. Strata and Cross-Stratum Interaction

The four strata deserve their own section because the strata structure is doing a lot of the framework's work, and because the framework's account of cross-stratum interaction is where it most substantially refines what existed before.

---

<a id="four-strata"></a>
### 4.1 The Four Strata

The framework recognises four strata, each defined by a characteristic *mode of pattern-realisation*. The strata are not arbitrary divisions of phenomena; they are genuinely different modes by which patterns hold themselves in being.

**Physical stratum.** Patterns here realise themselves through passive persistence under conservation constraints. A physical pattern is what it is by continuing to obey the structural conditions of physical existence — conservation of energy and momentum, thermodynamic constraints, geometric and topological invariances. Physical patterns do not actively maintain themselves; they persist because the conditions of their existence remain met, and they cease when the conditions cease.

**Biological stratum.** Patterns here realise themselves through active resistance to entropy via self-maintenance. A biological pattern continuously produces the conditions of its own persistence — it is not just constrained against decay, it actively counteracts decay. Biological patterns require physical substrate (they are made of matter obeying physical conditions) and modulate physical patterns (they organise matter into self-maintaining configurations that matter alone would not produce).

**Social stratum.** Patterns here realise themselves through multi-agent coordination. A social pattern requires multiple bearers in mutual orientation; it persists by being repeatedly enacted across agents who recognise one another's enactments as constituting the same pattern. Social patterns require biological substrate (they need agents capable of recognising one another and modifying their behaviour in response) and modulate biological patterns (they shape biological agents into coordinated forms that biological dynamics alone would not produce).

**Intellectual stratum.** Patterns here realise themselves through symbolic operation under structural conditions of coherence, correspondence, and revisability. An intellectual pattern holds itself in being by being articulated in transmissible form, tested against evidence and argument, and revised when warranted. Intellectual patterns require social substrate (they need shared symbolic systems and communicative agents) and modulate social patterns (they shape social arrangements by making them articulable, testable, and contestable).

---

<a id="strata-ordering"></a>
### 4.2 The Ordering of Strata

The four strata are ordered. The ordering is not arbitrary or conventional; it follows from the dependence relations among the modes of realisation.

Biological realisation presupposes physical realisation: there can be no self-maintaining entity without a physical substrate to maintain. Social realisation presupposes biological realisation: there can be no coordination among agents without agents whose biology supports the recognition and response that coordination requires. Intellectual realisation presupposes social realisation: there can be no symbolic articulation and testing without a community of agents holding shared symbolic systems.

The reverse does not hold. Physical realisation does not presuppose biological realisation; physical patterns exist where no biological patterns do. Biological realisation does not presuppose social realisation; biological patterns can be realised by single organisms without any social context. Social realisation does not presuppose intellectual realisation; many social patterns operate stably without articulation, justification, or testing.

The asymmetry of dependence is what gives the strata their ordering. Each stratum opens up a mode of realisation that the strata below cannot reach but that requires those strata's modes to be operating.

---

<a id="strata-precedence"></a>
### 4.3 Conditional Precedence

Higher strata have *conditional* precedence over lower strata. When a higher-stratum pattern is realising itself stably — when its own conditions of realisation are being met — its requirements take precedence over the requirements of the lower-stratum patterns it depends on.

The precedence is conditional in a strict sense: it applies only when the higher pattern is in fact realising itself stably. A higher-stratum pattern that is failing to meet its own conditions of realisation does not have precedence over the lower patterns that would otherwise serve as its substrate. The precedence is *earned* by realisation, not claimed by stratum.

This is the framework's reconstruction of Pirsig's moral hierarchy. The hierarchy is not a license for higher strata to override lower strata in all circumstances; it is a structural fact about which pattern's requirements come first when they conflict and both are realised. A failed intellectual pattern does not get to override a successful social pattern; a failed social pattern does not get to override a successful biological pattern. The precedence relation is one of conditional priority, not absolute dominion.

---

<a id="cross-stratum-modulation-dynamics"></a>
### 4.4 Cross-Stratum Modulation Dynamics

Cross-stratum modulation has its own dynamics that the framework should make explicit, because this is where the existing model's "level boundaries" understanding can be misread as forbidding what is in fact constitutive of how the strata actually interact.

*Higher patterns shape lower patterns by being realised.* When a biological pattern is realised, it shapes physical patterns into self-maintaining configurations — the configurations would not occur from physical dynamics alone. When a social pattern is realised, it shapes biological agents into coordinated behaviour — the behaviour would not occur from biological dynamics alone. When an intellectual pattern is realised, it shapes social arrangements by making them articulable and testable — the articulation would not occur from social dynamics alone. This shaping is modulation. It is real, it is constitutive, and it is permitted.

*Lower patterns constrain higher patterns by enabling or failing to enable them.* The physical conditions that biological patterns depend on can permit some biological patterns and forbid others. The biological capacities of agents can permit some social patterns and forbid others. The social conditions of communication can permit some intellectual patterns and forbid others. The constraint is real, but it is constraint on *what higher patterns can realise themselves*, not constraint on what those patterns *are*. A pattern that cannot be realised in current substrate conditions is not therefore non-existent — it is unrealised in those conditions and may be realisable in other conditions.

*Cross-stratum modulation can be supportive, distorting, or corrective.* Modulation is not automatically beneficial. A higher-stratum pattern can modulate a lower-stratum pattern in ways that support the lower pattern's own realisation (stabilising it, formalising it, making it more robust), in ways that distort the lower pattern's realisation (forcing it into configurations that compromise its mode of being), or in ways that correct it (surfacing where the lower pattern's profile-coherence is weak, without directly reshaping it). The kind of modulation is part of the higher pattern's profile — a high-quality higher pattern modulates supportively or correctively; a low-quality higher pattern distorts the substrate it depends on. Corrective modulation is most characteristic of intellectual patterns operating on lower strata, where testing representations against reality surfaces divergence without itself reshaping the lower pattern (see §2.4).

*Modulation is direct or cascaded.* Modulation at one stratum can propagate through multiple strata, but only the immediately-lower stratum is modulated *directly*; strata further below are reached through *cascade*. An intellectual pattern that modulates a social pattern can, through the social pattern's modulation of biological patterns, affect biology — but the effect on biology is cascaded, not direct, and the intellectual pattern does not thereby originate biological patterns. This distinction guards against a specific error: reading a higher pattern's distant-stratum effect as direct when it is in fact mediated through intervening strata. The cascade is mediated; the asymmetry of the substrate relation is preserved at every step (see §2.4 for the direct/cascaded distinction in full).

---

<a id="origination-vs-modulation"></a>
### 4.5 Origination is Forbidden; Modulation is Constitutive

The framework's account of cross-stratum interaction rests on a sharp distinction between origination and modulation. Origination is the act of bringing into existence; modulation is the act of shaping what is already there.

Origination across strata is forbidden because it would collapse the strata. If a higher stratum could originate lower-stratum patterns, then lower-stratum patterns would be products of higher-stratum activity, and the substrate relation would invert. The whole stratification would lose its structure. The MOQ commitment to non-reducibility across levels is the same point from the other direction: lower-stratum patterns also cannot originate higher-stratum patterns; the strata are mutually irreducible.

Modulation across strata is constitutive because without it the strata would be isolated. A model in which physical, biological, social, and intellectual patterns existed in parallel without interaction would be sterile — it would not describe the world we actually inhabit, in which higher-stratum patterns continuously shape the lower-stratum patterns they depend on, and lower-stratum patterns continuously condition the higher-stratum patterns they enable. The strata are a living system, and modulation is the medium of life within it.

This distinction — origination forbidden, modulation constitutive — is the framework's positive answer to what is sometimes called the problem of downward causation. The framework permits downward influence (modulation) while forbidding downward origination. The asymmetry is exact and the framework holds it sharply.

---

<a id="degraded-intellectual-realisation"></a>
### 4.6 Degraded Intellectual Realisation

The intellectual stratum has a feature the other strata do not share in the same way, and the framework should name it because it bears on how intellectual patterns are analysed.

A biological pattern that fails self-maintenance ceases to be a biological pattern — it dies, decomposes, returns to the physical stratum. A social pattern that fails coordination ceases to be that social pattern. But an intellectual pattern can fail the intellectual mode — fail correspondence, fail coherence, resist revision — and *continue operating at the intellectual stratum* in a degraded mode. It still uses symbolic operation; it still engages intellectual material; but it does not realise the intellectual mode in its full structural sense.

The framework therefore distinguishes *full intellectual realisation* (patterns genuinely operating under coherence, correspondence, and revisability — disciplined inquiry, mathematics, science and philosophy in their disciplined modes, truth as the central case) from *degraded intellectual realisation* (patterns that use intellectual substrate without realising the mode — sophistry, motivated reasoning that resists revision, confabulation that mimics correspondence without testing for it). Both are intellectual-stratum patterns; the framework analyses both. The profile-coherence of degraded-mode patterns shows specific weaknesses — in modulation responsibility, in internal compositional coherence, in field compatibility — that full-mode patterns do not show.

**A critical boundary.** Degraded intellectual realisation does *not* include propaganda or organised deception. This is the most important distinction in this subsection, and getting it wrong would violate the framework's commitment that stories, narratives, myths, and symbolic meaning are always social, never intellectual. Propaganda and organised deception are *social patterns* — their primary structural function is coordination through shared narrative — that *use* intellectual machinery (arguments, framings, the production of representations) as substrate. Their primary function operates at the social stratum: they produce coordinated belief and action through narratives that do not correspond to reality. The intellectual machinery employed in their production is substrate the social pattern recruits; it does not make the pattern intellectual.

The test for membership in degraded intellectual realisation is *primary structural function*, not surface machinery. Does the pattern's primary function operate at the intellectual stratum — testing, producing, or maintaining representations under (failed) intellectual conditions? Then it may be degraded intellectual realisation (sophistry's primary function is to produce argument that wins rather than tracks reality — an intellectual-mode failure). Does the pattern's primary function operate at a different stratum that merely uses intellectual machinery? Then it belongs to that stratum (propaganda's primary function is social coordination through false narrative — a social pattern using intellectual substrate). Surface machinery is not enough to locate a pattern at the intellectual stratum; the primary structural function is what locates it.

---

<a id="stratum-characteristic-work"></a>
### 4.7 Each Stratum's Characteristic Structural Work

A small refinement that the analysis of intellectual patterns surfaced. Each stratum has not only a characteristic *mode of becoming* (how its patterns realise themselves) but a characteristic *kind of structural work* (what its patterns' realisation produces in the world):

- Physical patterns *persist* — their work is endurance.
- Biological patterns *self-maintain* — their work is the active production of their own conditions of persistence.
- Social patterns *coordinate* — their work is binding multiple agents into shared structural form.
- Intellectual patterns *correct* — their work is testing representations against reality and revising when warranted.

The mode of becoming and the characteristic work are related (the mode makes the work possible) but distinct (the work is the output, the mode is the operating). This is why an intellectual pattern's downward modulation tends to be corrective (§2.4): correction is the intellectual stratum's characteristic structural work, and corrective modulation is that work applied to lower-stratum substrate. The formulation that "intellectual patterns correct rather than bind" names this: binding is the social stratum's work, correction is the intellectual stratum's, and conflating them is a stratum error.

<a id="section-5-quality-composition-fields"></a>
## 5. Quality, Internal Composition, and Field Effects

This section develops the framework's account of how quality is identified, how internal composition produces consonance and dissonance, how realisation varies in concrete circumstances, and how fields determine which patterns propagate and which collapse.

---

<a id="quality-detail"></a>
### 5.1 Quality, Developed

A pattern's quality is its profile-coherence — the integrated assessment of how the pattern fits into the relations it stands in. Five dimensions of profile-coherence can be distinguished, though they are aspects of a single property rather than separate scores.

**Substrate fit.** Does the pattern's realisation require substrate that is actually available and stable? A pattern whose realisation requires substrate that is fragile, depleting, or contested has reduced quality in this dimension, regardless of how well it does on other dimensions.

**Modulation responsibility.** When the pattern modulates lower-stratum patterns, does it support those patterns' realisations or distort them? A pattern that modulates its substrate destructively is, in this dimension, low quality — even if it appears to be realising itself well in the short term, it is undermining the conditions it depends on.

**Internal compositional coherence.** For composed patterns, do the constituent patterns mutually support each other's realisation (consonant composition), or do they interfere with each other's realisation (dissonant composition)? A pattern with consonant internal composition has structural integrity; a pattern with dissonant internal composition is internally undermining itself. For non-composed patterns, this dimension is not applicable.

**Field compatibility.** Does the pattern's field-propagation produce effects that the fields it propagates through can sustain? A pattern that propagates well only when enforced against opposition is field-incompatible with the fields it is in, and its quality in this dimension is reduced.

**Bearer adequacy.** Does the pattern require bearer modes that its actual bearers can sustain? A pattern that demands group-mode realisation from bearers who can only bear it in agent-mode is bearer-inadequate; a pattern that demands resource-intensive agent-mode realisation from agents who lack the capacity is similarly inadequate.

**Dispositional consistency.** Is the pattern's disposition coherent — do its tendencies under varying conditions cohere into a recognisable shape, or do they oscillate, contradict, or undermine each other? A pattern with incoherent dispositions is low quality in this dimension, because its realisation is itself unstable in form.

A pattern with high coherence across all applicable dimensions is high-quality without qualification. A pattern with mixed coherence is partial-quality: it has structural strengths and structural weaknesses, and a full description must say which is which. A pattern with poor coherence across multiple dimensions is low-quality, and its persistence (where it persists) depends on borrowed substrate, accumulated cost, enforcement, or the asymmetric benefit it provides to some bearers at the cost of others.

---

<a id="quality-not-aggregate"></a>
### 5.2 Quality is Not an Aggregate

Quality, in this framework, is not the average of separate scores. It is profile-coherence, which is a structural property of the *whole* profile.

A pattern with strong coherence in four dimensions and severe incoherence in one is not "mostly high-quality." It is a pattern whose realisation, taken as a whole, has a specific structural weakness — and the weakness may dominate the pattern's actual quality more than the strengths compensate for it. Whether the weakness dominates depends on the structural role of that dimension for that pattern.

Equally, a pattern with moderate coherence across all dimensions is not the same as a pattern with extreme coherence in some and weakness in others. The two have different profiles, and their structural behaviour differs accordingly. Profile-coherence is a description of the whole; reducing it to an aggregate loses what makes the description useful.

This is why the framework declines to score quality. The existing model's structural-axis profile and field-bearer matrix produce numerical outputs; the framework reformulates these as descriptions of profile-coherence whose value is in their structural detail, not in their scoring. Whether the existing scoring conventions are preserved, modified, or replaced is a downstream decision; the framework requires that whatever conventions are used preserve the profile-coherence as something analytically visible, not collapse it into a single number.

---

<a id="consonance-dissonance"></a>
### 5.3 Consonance and Dissonance — The Character of Internal Composition

When a pattern has internal compositional structure, the mutual relation among its constituents has structural character. The framework describes this character using **consonance** and **dissonance**.

A pattern's internal composition is **consonant** when its constituent patterns mutually support each other's realisation. Each constituent's realisation, in context with the others, produces conditions that strengthen the others' realisations. The composed pattern is held in being by the continuous mutual realisation of constituents whose individual realisations cohere into the composed pattern's form.

A pattern's internal composition is **dissonant** when its constituent patterns mutually interfere with each other's realisation. Each constituent's realisation, in context with the others, produces conditions that degrade the others' realisations. The composed pattern is still composed — it has a recognisable form — but its persistence depends on borrowed substrate or accumulated cost rather than on the mutual realisation strengthening itself.

The terms apply at the level of the composed pattern's internal composition, not as separate entities. A consonance is not a thing in the framework; it is a *property* of how a composed pattern's constituents stand in mutual relation. Likewise dissonance. The vocabulary survives because it is descriptively useful; it does not require a separate entity-category.

A composed pattern can be partly consonant and partly dissonant. Governance, for example, can have consonant relations among justice, authority, and legitimacy while having dissonant relations between any of these and a captured judiciary or corrupt enforcement apparatus. The pattern's overall character is then mixed — it is held together by the consonant constituents while being undermined by the dissonant ones. The framework's analysis of such patterns must surface both, not collapse them into a single verdict.

Patterns with predominantly consonant internal composition are higher-quality in their internal-compositional-coherence dimension. Patterns with predominantly dissonant internal composition are lower-quality in this dimension. This is a structural assessment, not a moral one; the framework reports the structure as it is.

---

<a id="bounded-persistence-dissonant"></a>
### 5.4 The Bounded Persistence of Dissonant Patterns

A pattern with predominantly dissonant internal composition can persist for a time. This persistence is real but bounded.

Cancer is a biological pattern with dissonant internal composition: its cells are individually realising themselves through the biological mode (active resistance to entropy through self-maintenance) but their realisations mutually interfere with the organism's larger biological patterns — disrupting tissue integrity, organ function, and ultimately the organism's substrate for sustaining the cancer itself. The pattern persists by drawing on the organism's substrate faster than it degrades that substrate; when the substrate is exhausted, the cancer collapses along with the organism.

Captured governance is a social pattern with dissonant internal composition: its constituents (formal authority, procedural mechanisms, enforcement structures) are individually present but their realisations interfere with each other — formal authority without legitimacy generates noncompliance, procedural mechanisms without integrity generate cynicism, enforcement without proportionality generates resistance. The pattern persists by drawing on inherited legitimacy, accumulated infrastructure, and the absence of immediate alternatives; when these are exhausted, the captured governance collapses.

In both cases, the persistence is real. The patterns are realising themselves. They have profiles, dispositions, and effects in the world. The framework does not declare them non-patterns because they are dissonant; it describes them honestly as the patterns they are, with attention to the bounded character of their persistence.

This is what allows the framework to analyse low-quality patterns rigorously without either moralising them out of existence or treating them as if their persistence proved their quality. Persistence and quality are not the same property. A pattern can persist for a time without being high-quality; the persistence is supported by something other than the pattern's own structural integrity. The framework surfaces what is supporting the persistence (borrowed substrate, accumulated cost, enforcement, asymmetric bearer-benefit) and notes that these supports are themselves bounded.

---

<a id="field-relative-detail"></a>
### 5.5 Field-Relative Value Effects

Within a field, the same pattern produces different effects depending on the bearer's position in the field and the field's constitution. The framework adopts the Circle/Ripple theory's *field-relative valuation* in this section, in adapted form.

A field has positions. Within a value field of the Circle/Ripple kind, positions include the internal (those whose value experience depends on the bearer), the allied (those with active committed engagement in the bearer's success), the neutral (those without active commitment), and the hostile (those whose interests run counter to the bearer). These are positions *within the field*, not separate fields — a single field has all of these positions, and the same pattern produces different effects from each position.

Position effects are objective within the field. The hostile position is not hostile because anyone interprets it as hostile; it is hostile because the structural relations between the bearer's realisations and that position's value field are antagonistic. The same is true of allied and neutral positions. The effects are field-relative, but within the field they are objective.

This is not relativism. Different fields produce different position effects for the same pattern, and within each field the effects are determinate. A relativism that said the pattern is good in some fields and bad in others would be missing the structural account: the pattern produces *real value effects* in each field, and the differences between fields are themselves structurally explicable from the fields' constitutions.

---

<a id="cross-field-structural-costs"></a>
### 5.6 Cross-Field Structural Costs

A pattern's field-relative effects within a single field are a snapshot — they describe what is happening in that field at the pattern's typical operation. But a pattern persists through time, and across many fields, and the *accumulation* of asymmetric effects across fields produces structural costs that no single field-snapshot can show.

A pattern that produces strong positive effects in allied fields, neutral effects in neutral fields, and strong negative effects in hostile fields has, in any single allied field, a profile that looks like high-quality realisation. But the accumulated cost of the hostile-field effects, over time and across the many hostile fields the pattern produces, generates a counter-pattern response: hostile fields organise against the pattern, allied fields must invest in defending against the counter-pattern, and the cycle escalates. The pattern's apparent local quality is contradicted by its structural instability across fields.

The framework names these *cross-field structural costs*. They live in the persistence-dynamics analysis of a pattern, not in any single field's snapshot. A full quality assessment must include both the field-snapshot dimensions and the cross-field structural costs — together they constitute the pattern's quality over time.

<a id="section-7-implications-limits-next"></a>
## 7. Implications, Limits, and What Comes Next

The framework is now constructed. This section names what the framework enables, what it does not enable, and what would have to be true for the framework to be operationalised in the stronger sense the original prompt gestured toward.

---

<a id="what-the-framework-enables"></a>
### 7.1 What the Framework Enables

The framework enables a unified description of value patterns that does not require separate accounts for stability and becoming, that handles cross-stratum interaction without confusion, that handles patterns at any compositional scale uniformly, and that gives quality a structural analytical handle without reducing it to a number.

In practice, this means:

A pattern described in this framework's vocabulary includes its stratum, its compositional scale, its constituents (if composed) and the character of their mutual relation (consonant, dissonant, or mixed), its substrate dependencies, its modulation relations, its field characteristics, its bearer modes, its dispositions, and the coherence among these. The description is a profile. The pattern's quality is in the profile's coherence.

Comparison between patterns becomes comparison between profiles. Two patterns at the same stratum can be compared by how their profiles cohere; the comparison is not a ranking but a structural distinction. Patterns at different compositional scales within a stratum can be compared with respect to how each contributes to and is constituted by the others.

Conflict between patterns becomes analysable through the strata's conditional precedence. When two patterns conflict, the framework asks: are they at the same stratum, or different? If different, is the higher pattern meeting its conditions of realisation? If yes, conditional precedence applies. If no, the precedence is not earned and the lower pattern's requirements may take priority.

Cross-stratum cascades become traceable. A modulation at one stratum produces effects through the strata it cascades through; the framework gives the vocabulary to follow the cascade without collapsing the strata.

Dysfunctional patterns become analysable on their own terms. A pattern with dissonant internal composition is not dismissed as a non-pattern; it is described as the pattern it is, with attention to what supports its bounded persistence and what makes the persistence bounded. The framework can analyse coercion, capture, cancer, corruption — patterns that exist and matter — with the same rigour it applies to high-quality patterns.

---

<a id="what-the-framework-does-not-enable"></a>
### 7.2 What the Framework Does Not Enable

The framework does not enable operationalisation of Dynamic Quality. This is by design and not a defect. Any framework that operationalised Dynamic Quality would have ceased to be a Pirsig-compatible framework. The framework's only term for the relation between Dynamic Quality and patterns is *passage*, and passage is recognised retrospectively, never operationalised.

The framework does not yet enable verifiable structural prediction. The original prompt mentioned "verifiable" structural statements about value behaviour as the long-range goal. The framework, as constructed, is descriptive grammar: it gives the vocabulary in which structural statements can be made. Whether those statements admit verification by others depends on (a) the precision of the profiles assigned to specific patterns, (b) whether the relations among primitives can be tightened from qualitative to semi-formal, and (c) whether the empirical anchors for profile-coherence are stable enough that two careful analysts would assign similar profiles to the same pattern.

The framework does not enable quantitative scoring of quality. Quality is profile-coherence, and profile-coherence is structural rather than numerical. Where scoring conventions are useful for compactness, they should be understood as analytical aids, not as quality measurements.

The framework does not enable replacement of Atlas entries by automated procedure. The framework is foundational vocabulary; refactoring existing Atlas entries to use it requires careful work entry-by-entry. The framework gives the grammar; the entries are sentences in the grammar.

---

<a id="what-would-be-true-for-operationalisation"></a>
### 7.3 What Would Be True if the Framework Were Operationalised

For the framework to become operational in the sense of supporting verifiable structural statements about value behaviour, several conditions would have to hold:

*Profile specifications would need to be precise enough that two careful analysts assign the same profile to the same pattern.* This requires both clearer rules for what counts as a complete profile and stable empirical anchors for the profile's elements. The framework provides the categories; operationalisation would require disciplined practice with them.

*The relations among primitives would need to be tightened from qualitative to semi-formal.* The framework currently states relations in prose. Operationalisation would require something like a small set of structural rules: under what conditions does substrate fail? what counts as supportive modulation vs distorting modulation? what propagation rules characterise specific fields? what counts as consonant vs dissonant internal composition? These rules would be empirical generalisations, not axioms.

*The framework would need to be stress-tested against multiple existing Atlas entries.* The test is whether the framework can re-describe what those entries describe, in its own vocabulary, without losing information or smuggling in new content. If many entries re-describe cleanly, the framework is operationally adequate. If many entries do not, the framework needs revision — or the entries do.

*Boundary conditions would need to be explicit.* For what kinds of patterns does the framework apply cleanly? For what kinds does it strain? The framework was constructed against the case of human-recognisable value patterns; whether it extends to value patterns we have not yet recognised, or to non-human bearers, is open.

These conditions describe what Task 3 would look like. Task 2 — this document — is the framework itself. Task 3 would be its operationalisation, conducted only after Task 2 has been stress-tested against the project's existing material.

<a id="relation-to-existing-model"></a>
### 7.4 Relation to the Existing Conceptual Model

The framework does not require any specific change to the existing conceptual model. It is foundational vocabulary that the model could be refactored against, but refactoring is not within scope here.

Where the framework differs from the existing model:

The existing model treats *laws* as structural axes that define a dimensional space, with Atlas entries as positions in that space. The framework treats laws — to the extent it uses the term — as descriptions of *conditions on realisation within a stratum*. This is a different conceptualisation; whether the existing model's law structure should be refactored or whether it can be preserved as one specific way of articulating realisation conditions is a separate decision.

The existing model treats *the structural axis profile* as a vector of scores against laws. The framework treats it as one specific way of making a pattern's profile-coherence analytically visible. The vector form may or may not be the best representation.

The existing model treats *the field-bearer matrix* as a specific table structure. The framework treats it as a specific way of making field-relative effects across bearer modes analytically visible.

The existing model's *agent/group bearer applicability* is preserved in the framework as a derived characterisation of patterns with respect to bearer modes.

The existing model's *level scoring* maps onto the framework's notion of where a pattern's primary mode of realisation occurs.

The existing model's *cross-field structural costs* in the most recent construction method is adopted directly.

The framework's introduction of compositional scale within a stratum may be the largest implication for the existing model. The current Atlas catalogues patterns at a relatively uniform compositional scale (individual virtues, individual coordination patterns). The framework permits — and may eventually require — Atlas entries at larger compositional scales (governance, religion, language, civilisation) and at smaller ones. Whether and how to expand the Atlas in this direction is a downstream decision.

<a id="relation-to-cluster-work"></a>
### 7.5 Relation to Cluster Work

The framework's commitment to a single entity-category (pattern, at all compositional scales) means that the project's existing *cluster* work fits naturally as either large-scale pattern entries in the Atlas (when the cluster has achieved enough stability and coherence to warrant a pattern entry of its own) or as exploratory essay work describing structural relationships between existing Atlas entries (when the cluster description is interpretive work-in-progress, not yet a pattern entry).

The framework does not introduce a separate *cluster* entity-category. Clusters that have achieved structural reality become large-scale pattern entries. Clusters that are still interpretive remain essays. The distinction between "Atlas entry" and "cluster essay" is a distinction of Canon-readiness, not of entity-category.

This is structurally cleaner than treating clusters as a separate kind of thing. It also matches the project's existing governance: the Canon Promotion Checklist applies to patterns; if a large-scale pattern (such as governance) earns Canon promotion, it becomes a Canon Atlas entry like any other.

<a id="dynamics-half-signpost"></a>
### 7.6 The Dynamics Half — Signpost Only

The framework as built is the *statics half*. It describes patterns as they stand: their composition, substrate, modulation, field relations, bearer modes, dispositions, persistence character, and profile-coherence. It answers *what a pattern is and how it is structured*.

There is a second half the framework does not yet contain: the *dynamics half*, concerned with *value-flow* — how value moves through the connections between patterns, how that movement shifts over time, how patterns connect, disconnect, divert, amplify, and starve one another, and on what timescales effects propagate. It answers *how value moves, and what happens when the movement changes*.

This subsection is a signpost, not a specification. The dynamics half has not been constructed, and nothing here should be read as committing the framework to particular dynamics primitives. What can be said is which territory the dynamics half must cover, because the statics work has repeatedly run up against its edge:

- The *temporal shape* of pattern manifestation — that some patterns are standing (continuously realised, like trust) while others are eruptive or sustained-covert (like betrayal in its various forms), and that a pattern's effects can propagate on timescales independent of the pattern's own manifestation.
- *Value-flow configurations* — diversion, capture, extraction, parasitism, betrayal — which are not standing patterns and not properties of the patterns they afflict, but descriptions of how value is moved or diverted through connections, typically mediated by a load-bearing agent.
- The *coupling* by which one pattern feeds another's value-flow — provisionally encountered in the analysis of power, whose defining operation (amplifying the patterns it is bound to) the statics half cannot fully express.

These observations have been recorded as they arose, held rather than forced into static structure. The dynamics half will require its own construction, comparable in scope to this one, and will draw on the Circle/Ripple theory's experiential notions (ripple, field, reserves, priming) as prior approaches to the same territory. It will need to hold the Dynamic Quality boundary with particular care: value-flow describes how value moves among existing patterns; it does not describe where new value comes from. That remains passage, and passage remains non-operationalised. The dynamics half is the framework's largest outstanding work, and it is deliberately left unspecified here.

<a id="closing-orientation"></a>
### 7.7 Closing Orientation

The framework is now built. Its claim is modest in one sense and ambitious in another.

The modest claim: the framework gives the project a coherent vocabulary for talking about value, value-becoming, stability, change, cross-stratum interaction, fields, internal composition, and quality, without requiring any single primitive to do more work than it can sustain. The vocabulary is small enough to be memorisable, structured enough to be useful, and disciplined enough to not collapse under the foundational constraint of Pirsig's metaphysics.

The ambitious claim: the framework dissolves the standard state/operator separation at the foundational level, treats patterns as having two aspects rather than being either states or operators or both at once, and treats patterns as existing at all compositional scales with a single entity-category. The first move opens a coherent path between formal analysis and Pirsig's metaphysics. The second move maintains structural parsimony across compositional scales — biology's solution adopted for value patterns generally.

What comes next is stress-testing. The framework should continue to be applied to existing Atlas entries, in the framework's own vocabulary, to see whether the entries can be re-described without loss and without strain. The two stress tests already conducted (Beauty and Justice) revealed that the framework holds against most cases and surfaces specific kinds of strain against others. The compositional-scale principle in this revision addresses the most significant strain found so far (the intra-stratum binding observed in Justice). Further stress tests will reveal whether the framework, as now constructed, holds against the next class of difficult cases.

This Task 2 revised deliverable is not Canon. It is Exploratory. Whether any part of it is eventually promoted to Canon depends on the same procedure that governs everything else in the project: stress-testing, scrutiny, explicit promotion, declaration. The framework has been constructed; whether it earns persistence remains to be determined.
