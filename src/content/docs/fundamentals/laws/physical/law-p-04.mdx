## L-P-04: Symmetry

**Statement:**
Physical laws remain invariant under certain transformations — and by Noether's theorem, every such symmetry corresponds to a conserved quantity.

---

### Function

Symmetry describes the structural consistency of physical laws across transformations of position, orientation, and time. A law is symmetric if it produces the same results regardless of where, in which direction, or when it is applied. Noether's theorem establishes that every such symmetry corresponds to a conserved quantity: translational symmetry yields conservation of momentum, rotational symmetry yields conservation of angular momentum, time-translation symmetry yields conservation of energy.

Symmetry is therefore not a secondary aesthetic feature of physical law — it is the structural condition from which conservation laws derive. L-P-01 and L-P-04 are not independent; conservation is grounded in symmetry. What is conserved is determined by which symmetries hold.

In MOQ terms, symmetry is the physical level's expression of structural coherence: the fact that the same pattern holds across contexts is what makes physical reality navigable, predictable, and capable of supporting higher-level patterns that depend on consistent physical behaviour.

---

### Cross-Level Grounding

Symmetry operates as a purely physical structural principle. Higher levels do not inherit physical symmetry directly. Biological systems exhibit structural symmetry that correlates with fitness in many species, but this is a convergent result of selection rather than a derivation from physical symmetry. Social and intellectual patterns exhibit analogous preferences for consistent application of rules, but these are level-appropriate structural properties, not consequences of physical symmetry.

The relationship between physical symmetry and higher-level structural consistency is one of resonance — similar structural logic appearing at different levels — not causal derivation.

---

### Failure Mode

Symmetry breaking at the physical level is not a failure — it is a productive physical process that generates structure from undifferentiated states. The crystallisation of matter from a uniform melt, the emergence of distinct particle types from a unified early-universe field, the breaking of electroweak symmetry in the early universe — these are symmetry-breaking events that produce higher-order physical organisation. The structure that physical reality exhibits at every scale above the most foundational is the result of successive symmetry-breaking events.

What can fail at the physical level is not symmetry itself but the conditions under which symmetry is identified or applied. Mistaking an approximate symmetry for an exact one produces predictions that fail at fine resolution; missing a symmetry that holds produces conservation laws being treated as brute facts when they are in fact derivative of symmetries that have not yet been recognised. These are failures of physical understanding rather than failures of symmetry as a structural axis.

---

### Observable Expressions

- Conservation of momentum from translational symmetry (Noether)
- Conservation of angular momentum from rotational symmetry (Noether)
- Conservation of energy from time-translation symmetry (Noether)
- Symmetry breaking in phase transitions (crystallisation, ferromagnetism)
- CPT symmetry in particle physics

---

### MOQ Placement

Symmetry is the structural ground of conservation at the physical level — it explains why conservation laws hold rather than asserting them as brute facts. By Noether's theorem, every conservation law is the consequence of a corresponding symmetry; what is conserved at the physical level is therefore determined by which symmetries the universe exhibits.

This makes symmetry the deepest of the physical structural axes in one specific sense: it grounds why other physical laws have the form they do. It does not by itself make the physical level a reliable substrate for higher-level patterns — that function belongs to lawlike regularity (L-P-05). Symmetry establishes what is preserved under transformation; lawlike regularity establishes that the same configuration produces the same outcome. Both are required for the physical level to function as the foundation MOQ describes, but each addresses a distinct structural condition.
