## L-B-02: Adapt

**Statement:**
Biological systems that cannot adjust their organisation or behaviour in response to changing conditions lose functional viability. Adaptation operates at two distinct levels: within an individual organism's lifetime through phenotypic plasticity, and across generations through evolutionary selection.

---

### Function

Adaptation describes two related but mechanistically distinct biological processes that must not be conflated:

**Phenotypic plasticity** operates within an individual organism's lifetime. The same genotype produces different phenotypes — body shapes, physiological responses, behavioural patterns — in response to environmental conditions. Seasonal changes in colouration, immune system calibration to local pathogens, and learned behavioural responses are all expressions of phenotypic plasticity. The genome does not change; the expression of it does.

**Evolutionary adaptation** operates at the population level across generations. Heritable variation within a population, combined with differential reproductive success under selection pressure, produces shifts in the frequency of traits over time. What adapts is not the individual organism but the population's pattern of variation. This process operates on generational timescales rather than within individual lifetimes.

Both forms of adaptation serve the same functional purpose: maintaining biological viability when environmental conditions shift. A biological pattern that can adjust its expression within a lifetime handles short-term variation; a population that accumulates heritable changes handles long-term directional change. Neither form of adaptation is superior — they operate at different scales and address different types of environmental challenge.

In MOQ terms, both phenotypic plasticity and evolutionary adaptation are static biological patterns. They are established mechanisms through which biological systems maintain viability across changing conditions — not responses to Dynamic Quality but functional properties of biological organisation selected for because they improve persistence.

---

### Cross-Level Grounding

Adaptation depends on the physical level for its substrate — genetic material, cellular mechanisms, and environmental conditions are all physical. It does not depend on social or intellectual patterns, though social species exhibit culturally transmitted behavioural adaptation that operates above the biological level and must be distinguished from biological adaptation proper.

Adaptation enables but does not generate social patterns. The capacity for learning, social bonding, and behavioural flexibility that biological adaptation produces creates the conditions within which social patterns can emerge — but the social level is not a direct product of biological adaptation.

---

### Failure Mode

Adaptation fails when the rate of environmental change exceeds the capacity of either phenotypic plasticity or evolutionary selection to keep pace. For phenotypic plasticity, this produces an organism whose regulatory responses are miscalibrated to conditions — chronic stress responses activated in non-threatening environments, immune systems tuned to pathogens no longer present, behavioural patterns that no longer match available resources. For evolutionary adaptation, a sufficiently rapid environmental shift can drive a population to extinction before selection can produce viable variants.

A distinct failure mode: adaptation to local conditions can reduce fitness under different conditions, producing evolutionary trade-offs rather than improvements. Adaptation is always relative to a selection environment, not absolute optimisation.

---

### Observable Expressions

**Phenotypic plasticity:**
- Immune system calibration to local pathogen environments
- Seasonal physiological changes in response to temperature and daylight
- Learned behavioural responses to environmental contingencies
- Developmental plasticity in response to nutritional conditions during growth

**Evolutionary adaptation:**
- Selection for antibiotic resistance in bacterial populations
- Beak morphology variation in response to available food sources
- Camouflage colouration under visual predation pressure
- Physiological adaptation to altitude across high-altitude human populations

---

### MOQ Placement

Adapt is the second biological law, operating within and dependent on the capacity for self-maintenance established in L-B-01. It establishes that biological viability requires not only active maintenance of a current pattern but the capacity to modify that pattern in response to conditions — at the individual level through plasticity and at the population level through selection. Both are static biological mechanisms, not expressions of Dynamic Quality.
