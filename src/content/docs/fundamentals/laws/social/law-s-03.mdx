## L-S-03: Reciprocity

**Statement:**
Any system of multiple coordinating agents whose interactions repeat over time requires predictable patterns of mutual obligation — exchange relationships that persist beyond single interactions — to avoid the prohibitive cost of negotiating every interaction from scratch.

---

### Structural Function

Reciprocity is the structural condition under which sustained multi-agent interaction becomes tractable. Without reciprocity, every interaction must be evaluated independently: what does each party give, what does each receive, are the terms acceptable, can the other party be trusted, what enforcement is available. The computational and energetic cost of negotiating every interaction from scratch exceeds what coordinating systems can sustain at any meaningful scale.

Reciprocity solves this by establishing patterns of expectation that persist across interactions. Three distinct forms operate together:

**Direct reciprocity** — A gives to B with the expectation that B will reciprocate to A in some future interaction. This is the simplest form, requiring sustained interaction between specific individuals.

**Generalised reciprocity** — A gives to B with the expectation that some member of the group will reciprocate to A in some future interaction. This scales beyond pairwise relationships and enables coordination among individuals who may not interact repeatedly.

**Indirect reciprocity** — A gives to B in part because doing so builds A's reputation, which produces reciprocation from third parties who observe or learn of A's behaviour. This scales further, enabling cooperation in large groups where most pairs of individuals never interact.

Together these three forms produce predictable patterns of mutual obligation that allow coordinating systems to operate at scales far exceeding what direct individual negotiation could sustain.

In MOQ terms, reciprocity is what makes complex social organisation possible. Hierarchy enables coordination; loyalty stabilises commitment; reciprocity makes the actual exchange of value within the group sustainable.

---

### Structural Universality

Reciprocity in this structural sense passes the universe test. Any multi-agent coordinating system involving repeated interactions and exchange of value among members must develop predictable patterns of mutual obligation, regardless of substrate or evolution path.

Evolutionary game theory (Axelrod's tournaments) has demonstrated that strategies based on reciprocity outperform both pure cooperation and pure defection across a wide range of conditions. Cross-cultural anthropological evidence (Henrich and colleagues' ultimatum game studies) shows fairness norms operating in every human society studied, with variation in form but consistency in structural requirement. Both findings suggest reciprocity is not a contingent feature of human social organisation but a structural requirement that any sustained multi-agent coordination must satisfy.

The original framing "I give so that you will give" captures direct reciprocity but misses generalised and indirect reciprocity, which are more important at larger scales.

---

### Failure Mode

The reciprocity mechanism fails in three directions:

**Insufficient reciprocity:** When patterns of mutual obligation are unreliable — gifts not returned, contributions not recognised, obligations not honoured — interactions revert to single-instance negotiations or cease entirely. The cost of participation exceeds the benefit. Members withdraw from exchange, reducing coordination to direct biological mechanisms or dissolving the group entirely.

**Asymmetric reciprocity:** When obligations flow predominantly in one direction over time, reciprocity transforms into structural exploitation. The pattern persists in form — exchanges still occur — but the value flow is one-directional. This is one of the more common failure modes in human institutions, where reciprocity language masks one-sided extraction.

**Rigid reciprocity:** When obligation patterns become so fixed that they cannot adjust to changing conditions, reciprocity stops serving coordination and becomes ritualised exchange that may even prevent more efficient cooperation. The form is preserved at the cost of the function.

### Atlas Coordination

Reciprocity as a structural axis is instantiated in human societies through multiple value patterns analysed in the Atlas:

- [Trust](/atlas/trust/) — voluntary risk acceptance based on expected reciprocity
- [Justice](/atlas/justice/) — formalised reciprocity as institutional principle
- [Honor](/atlas/honor/) — reputation-based mechanism for indirect reciprocity
- [Greed](/atlas/greed/) — value pattern that systematically violates reciprocity
- [Envy](/atlas/envy/) — value pattern arising from perceived reciprocity asymmetry

The relationship between reciprocity and other social structural axes is significant. Coercion can enforce hierarchy without requiring reciprocity, but groups that operate primarily through coercion lose the value-generating potential of voluntary exchange. The presence or absence of reciprocity is one of the strongest indicators of social pattern quality.

---

### Observable Expressions

- Direct reciprocity in pairwise relationships across humans and other social species
- Generalised reciprocity in tribal and small-community contexts (gift economies)
- Indirect reciprocity through reputation in human social networks of any scale
- Cross-cultural fairness norms documented in ultimatum game studies (Henrich et al)
- Symbiotic and mutualistic exchange in non-human biological systems (precursor mechanism)

---

### MOQ Placement

Reciprocity is the third social structural axis. It establishes the exchange condition without which sustained multi-agent interaction becomes prohibitively costly. Together with hierarchy (asymmetric authority) and loyalty (commitment over time), reciprocity completes the foundational structural triad on which all other social patterns depend.
