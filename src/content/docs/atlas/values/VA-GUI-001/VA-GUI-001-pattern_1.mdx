## Guilt — Narrative Articulation

### Character of the Pattern

Guilt is the social pattern by which an agent regulates their own conduct against the active value patterns they are embedded in, through internalised recognition that one of their own actions or omissions was incompatible with those patterns. It is intra-agent in its operation but constituted by reference to the value patterns operating in the agent's relational field — a solitary creature embedded in no value patterns cannot have guilt no matter how much capacity for self-directed pressure it possesses. The value patterns are sometimes codified into formal norms (laws, professional standards, religious commandments), but guilt operates on the underlying value patterns directly, whether they have been codified or not. A friend who lets a friend down feels guilt without any formal norm having been articulated: the friendship is a value pattern, the action was incompatible with what the friendship was, and that incompatibility is what the guilt registers.

The structural sequence is: recognition of one's own act as incompatible with the value patterns one is embedded in, self-administered evaluative pressure directed at oneself for the act, orientation toward repair or restraint, and — when repair is achieved — restoration of alignment with the value patterns. The pattern is action-oriented: the pressure attaches to what one did and what one could do about it, not to what one is. This is the structural distinction from shame, which attacks identity rather than action and orients toward identity-protection rather than repair.

Guilt is pre-justificatorily recognisable. An agent in the operation of guilt recognises the pattern in their own experience prior to any theory of moral responsibility, much as a subjected party recognises coercion. This marks guilt as a genuine social-stratum pattern rather than a theoretical construct.

### Why Social is Primary, Despite Strong Biological Substrate

Guilt engages biological substrate intensely — cortisol response, autonomic arousal, behavioural inhibition, appeasement gestures. The biological dimension is so prominent that one might initially place guilt at the biological stratum. But the biological mechanisms are substrate, not the pattern: the same mechanisms fire in fear, shame, and ordinary stress. What makes guilt *guilt* is the value-pattern-referenced recognition of one's own action as incompatible with the patterns one is embedded in, and value patterns at this level — reciprocity, loyalty, care, role-bearing, integrity, honor — are social. Without a social field of active value patterns, the biological mechanisms can fire but they do not constitute guilt.

The same discipline applies to intellectual engagement. Guilt can engage with abstract principles, theoretical articulations of moral responsibility, and reflective examination. But it operates in cultures and persons without explicit theoretical articulation — folk guilt is real guilt. Intellectual articulation of guilt (philosophical theories of responsibility, restorative justice frameworks, religious moral systems, and the codification of value patterns into formal norms) modulates guilt from above, correctively and supportively, but does not constitute it.

So: primary social. Biological substrate is intense but substrate. Intellectual engagement is modulation but does not constitute.

### The Adjacent Patterns and Their Distinctions

Guilt sits in territory closely adjacent to several other patterns, and the framework relies on internal-composition distinctions to keep them separate.

Shame shares with guilt the internal evaluative pressure but differs in target: shame is directed at identity ("I am bad"), guilt at action ("I did wrong"). Shame's orientation is identity-protection or hiding; guilt's is repair. The constituents differ — shame includes identity-level devaluation as a constituent; guilt includes orientation toward repair. They co-occur often in real experience but are structurally distinct.

Fear shares the biological substrate (stress, arousal, inhibition) but lacks the same constituent structure. Fear responds to threat without value-evaluation; guilt responds to one's own action as incompatible with the value patterns one is embedded in. An agent can feel fear of consequences without feeling guilt about what produced them.

Punishment is externally imposed sanction; guilt is internally administered pressure. The two relate but are not the same pattern. Punishment can sometimes induce guilt (when the punished party internalises the punisher's evaluation as legitimate) and guilt sometimes invites punishment (when an agent seeks external sanction to relieve internal pressure), but punishment is a coercion-adjacent social pattern operating on the agent, not the agent's own pattern.

Embarrassment is minor value-pattern deviation without moral gravity. It shares the recognition-and-pressure structure but operates at a much lower magnitude and on value patterns whose deviation does not register as moral.

These distinctions are recorded as relation records (guilt's adjacency-relations to shame, fear, embarrassment, punishment), each with a note that the `distinction` relation type (flagged provisional in the schema) would handle these cleanly.

### Guilt as a Cost-Source for Coercion

The structural feature of guilt that most repays the framework's analytical apparatus is its role as one of coercion's cost-sources. The corrected coercion entry records that coercion can leverage guilt to supply the credible contingent cost it operates through (REL-SUB-COE-GUI-001). When this leveraging occurs, an external party manipulates the agent's guilt — manufacturing it, invoking it, or amplifying it — so that the agent's own self-administered pressure becomes the cost the external party uses to secure compliance.

Two things are crucial here, structurally. First, guilt-leveraged coercion is sometimes more reliable than power-leveraged coercion, precisely because the cost is administered by the subjected party to themselves rather than requiring continued external enforcement. The bearer maintains the pressure even when the external party is absent. Second — and this is the analytical move that protects guilt's own profile — *guilt is not malfunctioning when it is being leveraged*. The pattern operates exactly as it always does: recognition of violation, self-administered pressure, orientation toward repair. The coercion is operating on guilt as substrate; it is not a degradation of guilt as a pattern.

This is why "manipulative guilt induction" does not appear in guilt's degradation conditions and is not part of guilt's own profile. The misattribution would smuggle a value-flow configuration (coercion using guilt) into the static profile of guilt. Under the framework, that material lives in the coercion-side cost-source relation, where it belongs. Guilt's own analysis remains clean.

What guilt's degradation conditions do contain are the modes where guilt itself malfunctions: chronic guilt without repair pathway (the pressure has nowhere to discharge), guilt attached to low-quality value patterns (the pattern restores embeddedness in patterns that may be degrading), and disproportionate pressure (the magnitude has failed). These are about guilt's own operation, not about other patterns operating on it.

### Empirical Recurrence

Guilt recurs across psychological, anthropological, and behavioural research. The distinction between guilt and shame is well-supported empirically: guilt-proneness predicts reparative action and prosocial behaviour, while shame-proneness predicts withdrawal and defensive aggression. Confession reduces stress in the bearer when it is met with the possibility of repair. Restorative-justice practices across cultures emphasise restitution over humiliation, drawing on the guilt-rather-than-shame structure. Evolutionary behavioural models depend on mechanisms that discourage cheating and stabilise reciprocity; guilt is one such mechanism.

The empirical observations support guilt's structural function as a low-cost enforcement mechanism: internal pressure performs correction work that would otherwise require external surveillance. Across observed contexts, where guilt is well-developed, external enforcement requirements are lower; where guilt is absent or weak, external enforcement requirements rise to compensate.

### What This Entry Holds for the Dynamics Half

Several features of guilt belong to value-flow dynamics rather than to guilt's static profile and are held here as observations awaiting the dynamics layer.

The "live" persistence of guilt in an agent over time — the pressure holding until repair occurs — has a temporal character that the static persistence_character field can name but not model. Guilt does not simply exist or not exist; it builds, holds, discharges, or accumulates. The temporal shape is part of how guilt operates.

The leveraging of guilt by external parties (in coercion) is itself a value-flow phenomenon: another pattern diverting and amplifying the agent's own self-administered pressure for its own purposes. This is structurally analogous to the betrayal and parasitism configurations the framework has previously held for the dynamics half. The static schema captures it as a cost-source relation; the full dynamics — how the leveraging operates over time, how it propagates, how it cascades into the bearer's relationships — awaits the dynamics half.

The repair-and-restitution cycle by which guilt resolves itself is a flow phenomenon: pressure builds in the bearer, motivates repair action, the repair-action restores value to the wronged party, the restored value redeems the bearer's standing, and the pressure discharges. The static entry records this as a disposition (orients toward repair) and as a persistence condition (persistence depends on repair pathway availability), but the full flow architecture of guilt-repair belongs to the dynamics half.

### Relations Recorded Separately

Guilt's structural relations to other patterns are captured as relation records, not restated here. They include: substrate dependence on biological capacity for stress and self-inhibition, on the social substrate of the active value patterns the agent is embedded in; supportive modulation of biology (when operating natively); supportive modulation of reciprocity and accountability in the social field; corrective and supportive modulation of guilt by intellectual patterns (theories of responsibility, restorative justice frameworks, codification of value patterns into norms); distinction-relations to shame, fear, embarrassment, and punishment (commonly conflated but structurally distinct); the cost-source relation from coercion (recorded on the coercion side as REL-SUB-COE-GUI-001, not duplicated here); and field-relative effects across the bearer's own position (internal), allied positions (wronged parties capable of accepting repair), and observing positions (neutrals reading accountability signals). Each is its own record in the relation layer.
